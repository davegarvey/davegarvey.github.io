# LinkedIn promotion

Prepare one publication-ready LinkedIn post after the article itself is final. The post
should help the right reader decide whether the article is worth their time. Treat the
article's readership and the trust behind Dave's voice as more important than reach.

## Write the post

The post should start a conversation around the article's most interesting idea, rather
than retell the article. Offer a draft as a starting point; Dave usually rewrites it in
his own words. When he does, review his version against this guide, fix errors and
missing links, and keep any further changes light.

- Before writing, find the article's sharpest question, tension, surprising implication
  or assumption a reader might challenge. Use that to shape the opening. A direct
  question often works, but don't make every post a question or force a hook the article
  hasn't earned.
- Keep the opening concise and specific enough to make the reader pause. Follow it with
  at most one or two short sentences of context when needed. Leave the explanation and
  evidence to the article; create curiosity without giving its whole argument away.
- Use a natural, thoughtful voice in restrained British English. Avoid an abstract-like
  introduction, a list of what the article covers, generic AI phrasing, marketing
  language, manufactured controversy, engagement bait, and calls to comment or react
  for reach. Don't inflate the stakes or promise more than the article delivers.
- Make the post and its claims understandable on their own, while giving a reader a
  reason to follow the direct article URL. The post need not state the article's full
  conclusion first.
- Put the direct article URL in the post text. Don't write “link in comments” or make the
  reader hunt for it. A direct link is clear about where it goes and lets readers choose
  whether to follow it.
- Use hashtags only when they add useful subject context, and keep them restrained. Don't
  tag people or organisations to borrow their audience; tag only when the article is
  meaningfully about them and the tag helps the reader. Emojis are unnecessary unless one
  feels natural in Dave's voice.
- Don't automate likes, comments, connection requests or other interaction. Don't use
  tactics whose purpose is to trigger distribution signals rather than serve readers.

## Choose the image treatment

Decide whether the post is clearer with LinkedIn's link preview or with a native image.
The link preview is the default: it carries the article title, description and share card
beside the direct URL. Suggest an optional native image only when it adds useful context
or communicates the article's idea better in the feed. An attached image replaces the
link preview, so account for the title and context the reader will lose.

Reuse an existing article image when it works at feed size and remains understandable
without the article around it. If a new native image is warranted, follow the image
production, dimensions, storage and alt-text instructions in `AGENTS.md` under “Assets
for sharing”. Always provide descriptive alt text for a native image.

Check the article's Open Graph title, description, canonical URL and image before
recommending the link preview. The repository's share-metadata check validates the built
metadata; use LinkedIn's Post Inspector to refresh or inspect a preview when useful.

## Keep platform advice current

These editorial standards are durable: be honest, useful, clear and respectful of the
reader's attention. LinkedIn's ranking and presentation can change. Treat claims about
what the algorithm rewards, ideal posting times, formats or reach as provisional. Check
current LinkedIn guidance or direct evidence before changing the workflow, and don't turn
uncertain reach statistics into requirements. A post's success is not a reason to adopt
a tactic that feels inauthentic or obscures the link.

Prepare the draft and image suggestions for Dave's review. Human approval is required
before anything is published to LinkedIn.

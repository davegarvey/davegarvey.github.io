I listened to this episode of The Ezra Klein Show today: **[Is There an A.I. Bubble? A Debate.](https://www.nytimes.com/2026/10/16/opinion/ezra-klein-podcast-azeem-azhar-paul-kedrosky.html)** I really enjoyed it. Klein's guests are Paul Kedrosky, a venture capitalist at SK Ventures and a fellow at MIT, who thinks AI is a bubble, and Azeem Azhar, a technology entrepreneur and analyst behind Exponential View, who sees a productive boom that's starting to show warning signs. Both bring strong arguments and plenty of data, and what they say makes a lot of sense, even though they come down on opposite sides.

<!--more-->

I found myself agreeing with both because they're often answering different questions. Kedrosky asks whether the labs can earn enough from their customers to justify what they're worth. Azhar asks whether all the computing being built will get used. You can say no to the first and yes to the second. Where they really clash is over the money: the debt, the valuations, and whether trouble at the labs would turn into a crash.

What resonated with me:

- **It's a race no one can leave.** Kedrosky describes the labs as stuck in a prisoner's dilemma: each might like to slow down, but none can while the others carry on.

  *My take:* so far, Dario Amodei's call last month to "pace the frontier" hasn't changed that. New models and tools are still arriving as fast as ever. And I can't see how an agreement to slow down could work, when every lab, and every country, would have to sign up and stick to it.

- **Most people don't need the frontier.** Kedrosky warns against reading too much into the soaring number of tokens. More tokens doesn't mean more money for the labs, because what they earn depends on the price as well as the volume. The price per token is falling fast, and most people are happy with cheaper models.

  *My take:* for everyday questions, cheaper models are already good enough, and that's where much of the volume has gone. In OpenRouter's [published usage data](https://openrouter.ai/data/newsletter/the-token-state-of-play), open-weight models have carried most of the tokens since July, more than 62%, while closed models still take 73% of the money spent.

- **The computing will be needed whoever wins.** Azhar argues that if companies switch to cheaper models, the labs lose out, but the demand for computing just moves elsewhere.

  *My take:* the data centres get used whichever model wins. The labs mostly rent their computing from the cloud providers. Companies that switch to open-weight models will mostly use the same cloud providers too, whether self-hosted or SaaS.

**Update:** Kedrosky wrote [a short post](https://paulkedrosky.com/ezra-klein-appearance-is-there-an-ai-bubble/) about his appearance on the show. In it, he says that getting caught up in whether AI actually works, or is useful, misses the point. He accepts that it does work and can be very useful. What he cares about is the huge amount of money being spent on it, and what that means for the wider economy. Then, that same week, Anthropic told prospective investors that its revenue grew 45% in the third quarter, after more than doubling in the second. The numbers are still huge, but it's a [sharp slowdown](https://www.exponentialview.co/p/anthropic-the-agony-and-the-ecstasy).
